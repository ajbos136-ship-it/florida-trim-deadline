The deadline on your TRIM notice is shorter than you think

It runs from the day the notice was mailed, not the day you opened it. Every claim on this page names the statute it came from, so you can check it yourself.

The Notice of Proposed Property Taxes goes out every August and most of them end up in a drawer. It is not a bill. It is the county's opinion of what your property is worth, produced in volume by an office that values tens of thousands of parcels without walking through any of them. Opinions produced that way contain mistakes, and Florida law gives you a cheap, formal way to challenge one.

What is a TRIM notice?

TRIM stands for Truth in Millage. The Notice of Proposed Property Taxes is the letter every Florida property owner gets in August, and it does three things: it tells you what the Property Appraiser thinks your property is worth as of January 1, it tells you what each taxing authority proposes to charge, and it starts a clock.

It is not a bill. The bill comes in November. This is the only document that tells you the number your bill will be calculated from, at the only point where you can still argue about it.

When are Florida TRIM notices mailed?

They go out in August, and the exact date is set by each county's Property Appraiser rather than by a single statewide date. That is why no honest guide can print the date for you. The date that matters is the mailing date printed on your own notice, and it is the date every deadline below counts from.

The clock, and where it actually starts

For a petition about your property's value, Florida Statute 194.011(3)(d) says a petition must be filed:

“at any time during the taxable year on or before the 25th day following the mailing of notice by the property appraiser”

Fla. Stat. § 194.011(3)(d)

Following the mailing. Not following receipt, and not following the day you got round to opening it. Find the mailing date printed on your own notice and count from there. If it sat unopened for a week, that week is gone.

There are two deadlines, and people file under the wrong one every year

What you are challengingYour deadline
The value the county put on your property25 days from the mailing of the TRIM notice
A denied exemption or classification30 days from the mailing of that denial notice

Those are two different notices with two different dates. Most short guides flatten the distinction, and it is the kind of error that is only discovered when it is too late to fix.

Calling the Property Appraiser does not stop the clock

This is the trap that costs the most people their appeal, and it does not feel like a trap at all.

An informal conference with the Property Appraiser's office is available and is often genuinely useful. Statute 194.011(2) says plainly that it is not a prerequisite to review. What the statute does not say is that it buys you time. It grants no extension, and on the question of tolling the deadline it is simply silent. The 25 days keep running from the mailing either way.

So every year, people have a pleasant phone call, are told somebody will get back to them, wait politely, and watch the window close.

File first, then have the conversation. You can always withdraw a petition. You cannot un-miss a deadline.

The fee: check your own county, and be careful what you read

Statute 194.013 sets a ceiling, not a price:

“a filing fee … in an amount determined by the board not to exceed $50 for each separate parcel”

Fla. Stat. § 194.013

Each county board picks its own fee under that cap. Sumter, Lake and Marion all charge $50. Some counties charge less, and Miami-Dade charges $15 per folio, which is lawful and current.

That matters because there are confident, current guides online stating that Florida law caps the fee at $15 per parcel, and citing this very statute for it. The figure is real in some counties. It is not the cap, and it is not what you will be writing a cheque for in Sumter, Lake or Marion. Read the fee off the clerk's own page in your county, and note the date you checked it.

Does this apply in my county?

The statutes on this page are Florida law and apply in all sixty-seven counties. The 25 and 30 day clocks, the informal conference, the evidence exchange, the payment rule and the January 1 valuation date do not change when you cross a county line.

The filing fee does. Statute 194.013 sets a ceiling and each county board picks its own number under it, so that one figure has to come from your own clerk. The three counties named on this page are the ones whose fees were verified directly.

Ask for the property record card. It is free, and it is where these are won

You are entitled to the county's own file on your property. Under Statute 194.011(4), when evidence is exchanged before a hearing the Property Appraiser must supply their own material plus the property record card, and their failure to comply on time reschedules the hearing. You can also simply ask the office for it.

Read it against your actual house. A wrong square footage, a wrong bedroom or bathroom count, a wrong year built, a wrong condition grade, a garage that was converted years ago, a pool that is not there. A documentable error is not a difference of opinion, and that distinction is the difference between an appeal that works and an argument about whether your house feels overvalued.

The rule almost nobody knows, and it kills petitions in April

Statute 194.014 applies to a petitioner who challenges the assessed value of property, and it requires two payments before the taxes become delinquent: all of the non-ad valorem assessments, and at least 75 percent of the ad valorem taxes, less the applicable discount under Statute 197.162.

“The value adjustment board must deny the petition by written decision by April 20 if the petitioner fails to make the payment required by this subsection.”

Fla. Stat. § 194.014(1)(c)

Note the two halves. It is easy to remember “75 percent” and forget that the non-ad valorem assessments have to be paid in full.

This one is brutal because of when it lands. You file in August, you wait months, you go to a hearing, and the thing that ends it is a payment deadline in the following spring that nobody mentioned. Filing a petition is not a reason to stop paying the bill.

What to do this week

  1. Find your notice and find the mailing date printed on it. Write the deadline on a calendar somewhere you will see it.
  2. Work out which of the two deadlines is yours: value, or a denied exemption. They are different notices with different dates.
  3. Ask the Property Appraiser's office for your property record card. Free, and you want it in hand before you decide anything.
  4. Read it against your actual house and write down anything that is factually wrong.
  5. Check the filing fee with your own county clerk rather than with a guide, and note the date you checked.
  6. If you are going to file, file. The form is DR-486, from the Florida Department of Revenue. Have the informal conference afterwards.

Common questions

Is it worth appealing at all?
Sometimes it is genuinely not, and that is worth knowing before you spend a weekend on it. If you have a homestead exemption, the Save Our Homes cap under Statute 193.155(1) limits your assessed value to the lower of 3 percent or CPI growth a year, and your assessed value can already sit well below market. Winning a reduction in market value does not always move the number your bill is calculated on. Work that out first.
What is the county's starting position?
Under Statute 194.301 the assessment carries a presumption of correctness, and the statute sets out what it takes to overcome it. You are not walking in level. That is an argument for evidence rather than opinion.
What date is my property actually valued as of?
January 1 of the tax year, under Statute 192.042. A sale down the road in September is not evidence about January 1, which surprises people.
Who hears it?
Under Statute 194.035, counties over 75,000 population must appoint special magistrates, so in Sumter, Lake and Marion your hearing is before one rather than before the board itself.
