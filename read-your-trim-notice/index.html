How to read your TRIM notice

Your notice was written by a statute, sentence by sentence. Read it that way and it tells you something most people miss: which of your two complaints the Value Adjustment Board can actually hear.

Everything below is quoted from section 200.069, Florida Statutes — the 2026 text, read on the Legislature’s own site on 22 August 2026. If you already know what your notice says and you are counting days, go straight to the 25-day deadline. If you have the petition in front of you, go to form DR-486, part by part.

Why every TRIM notice in Florida looks the same

Because it has to. Section 200.069 does not describe the notice in general terms; it prescribes it. The headings are listed. Several whole sentences are set out in the statute in capital letters, and the county copies them across. The statute then closes the door:

“Notwithstanding the provisions of s. 195.022, no county officer shall use a form other than that provided herein.”

§ 200.069, Fla. Stat.

A county may deviate only by paying for it and getting prior written permission from the executive director of the Department of Revenue — and even then it may not use a form “the substantive content of which is at variance with the form prescribed by the department.”

There is also a limit on what else can be stuffed in the envelope. The property appraiser

“may not include in the mailing of the notice of ad valorem taxes and non-ad valorem assessments additional information or items unless such information or items explain a component of the notice or provide information directly related to the assessment and taxation of the property.”

§ 200.069, Fla. Stat.

So this is a document you can read closely and trust to be the same document your neighbour got. That is unusual, and it is worth using.

Page one: seven columns, and none of them are your appeal

The first page opens with the words the statute puts there in subsection (1):

NOTICE OF PROPOSED PROPERTY TAXES
DO NOT PAY — THIS IS NOT A BILL

“The taxing authorities which levy property taxes against your property will soon hold PUBLIC HEARINGS to adopt budgets and tax rates for the next year.”

§ 200.069(1), Fla. Stat.

Then subsection (2)(a) gives the seven column headings, in this order and in these words:

#Heading, verbatimWhat it is
1“Taxing Authority”Who is charging you. The county, the school levies, your city or municipal service taxing unit, the water management district, any special districts, and any voter-approved debt.
2“Your Property Taxes Last Year”What you were actually levied last year.
3“Last Year’s Adjusted Tax Rate (Millage)”Not last year’s rate. The rolled-back rate — see below.
4“Your Taxes This Year IF NO Budget Change Is Adopted”Your new assessment at the rolled-back rate.
5“Tax Rate This Year IF PROPOSED Budget Is Adopted (Millage)”The rate they are actually proposing.
6“Your Taxes This Year IF PROPOSED Budget Change Is Adopted”The number most people read first, and the one they get angry about.
7“A Public Hearing on the Proposed Taxes and Budget Will Be Held:”Date, time and place. This is where column 6 gets decided, and you are allowed to turn up.

The renumbering that confuses everybody

Subsection (5) then relabels three of those seven. Under the totals row, the second, fourth and sixth columns are labelled Column 1, Column 2 and Column 3. So “Column 3” on your notice is the sixth column on the page. Directly below, the statute requires, in boldfaced type: “SEE REVERSE SIDE FOR EXPLANATION.”

Turn it over. That reverse side carries the most important sentence on the whole notice, and almost nobody reads it.

The sentence that decides whether you have an appeal

Subsection (8) sets out the explanation printed on the back of page one. Here is the entry for Column 3, in full:

“This column shows what your taxes will be this year under the BUDGET ACTUALLY PROPOSED by each local taxing authority. The proposal is NOT final and may be amended at the public hearings shown on the front side of this notice. The difference between columns 2 and 3 is the tax change proposed by each local taxing authority and is NOT the result of higher assessments.”

§ 200.069(8), Fla. Stat.

Read that twice. The state has written into the statute a sentence whose entire job is to tell you that the increase you are looking at on the front page is a spending decision, not a valuation decision.

And Column 2 is the control:

“This column shows what your taxes will be this year IF EACH TAXING AUTHORITY DOES NOT CHANGE ITS PROPERTY TAX LEVY. These amounts are based on last year’s budgets and your current assessment.”

§ 200.069(8), Fla. Stat.

Column 2 already contains your new assessment. That is what makes the comparison work: hold the rate still, change only the value, and column 2 is the result. Anything on top of that, in column 3, is the budget.

So which complaint do you have?

“My property is not worth that.”

Or: my exemption is missing, or my agricultural classification was denied.

That is a Value Adjustment Board petition, and it is on the clock from the day the notice was mailed. It lives on page two of the notice, not page one.

“Column 3 is too high.”

The taxing authority is proposing to spend more.

The VAB cannot help you and will not hear it. Your remedy is the public hearing whose date, time and location are printed in the seventh column, and the ballot box.

Both can be true at once

An assessment can be too high and a millage increase can be proposed in the same year. They are two separate fights, on two different calendars, decided by two different bodies. The petition deadline will pass long before some of those budget hearings finish. If you have the valuation complaint, file on that clock and argue the budget separately.

Page two: the page your appeal is actually about

Subsection (6)(a) requires the second page to state the parcel’s market value, and then for each taxing authority that levies against it:

“The assessed value, value of exemptions, and taxable value for the previous year and the current year.”

“Each assessment reduction and exemption applicable to the property, including the value of the assessment reduction or exemption and tax levies to which they apply.”

§ 200.069(6)(a), Fla. Stat.

Three different numbers, and people mix them up constantly. Market value is what the appraiser says it would sell for. Assessed value is market value after caps and reductions such as Save Our Homes. Taxable value is assessed value after exemptions come off. A Value Adjustment Board petition on value attacks the first one; a petition on a denied exemption attacks the step between the second and the third.

The reverse of that page, under subsection (6)(b), “shall contain definitions and explanations for the values included on the front side.” Free glossary, already in your hand.

Where your deadline is printed

Subsection (7) requires this statement to appear after the values on the front of page two, and this is the paragraph you are looking for:

“If you feel that the market value of your property is inaccurate or does not reflect fair market value, or if you are entitled to an exemption or classification that is not reflected above, contact your county property appraiser at (phone number) or (location).

If the property appraiser’s office is unable to resolve the matter as to market value, classification, or an exemption, you may file a petition for adjustment with the Value Adjustment Board. Petition forms are available from the county property appraiser and must be filed ON OR BEFORE (date).”

§ 200.069(7), Fla. Stat. Parenthesised items are blanks the county fills in.

Two things follow from that, and both are practical.

  1. Your deadline is a printed date, on your own notice, in that sentence. Not a date you calculate, not a date from a guide, not the date your neighbour in another county has. Find that line and read the date off it. Our deadline page explains the 25-day rule the county used to arrive at it, and has a calculator to check the county’s arithmetic — but the printed date is the one that governs you.
  2. The statute puts the property appraiser’s phone number in front of the petition, deliberately. The informal conference is the cheap path and it resolves a real share of these. Section 194.011(2) makes clear it is not a prerequisite. It also does not extend anything — so book it early, and file the petition on the printed date regardless of how the conversation is going.

Three smaller things the statute puts on the page

The rolled-back rate, which is not last year’s rate

Column three of the seven is headed “Last Year’s Adjusted Tax Rate (Millage)”, and subsection (2)(b) defines the term: it “means the rolled-back rate calculated pursuant to s. 200.065(1).” The rolled-back rate is the millage that would raise the same revenue as last year from the same properties. When values rise, it falls. That is the whole point of it — it is the baseline that makes a tax increase visible even in a year when nobody touched the rate.

Why your bill will be bigger than any column here

Subsection (9) requires the bottom of the notice to read, in bold conspicuous print:

“Your final tax bill may contain non-ad valorem assessments which may not be reflected on this notice such as assessments for roads, fire, garbage, lighting, drainage, water, sewer, or other governmental services and facilities which may be levied by your county, city, or any special district.”

§ 200.069(9), Fla. Stat.

These are charged per unit, not by value. A Value Adjustment Board petition about your market value does not touch them. In some counties they are also printed on the notice under a partition the statute describes as “a bold, horizontal line approximately 1/8-inch thick” — if you can see that line, the assessments below it are already listed for you.

The discount that is not shown

The reverse of page one carries a note: “Amounts shown on this form do NOT reflect early payment discounts you may have received or may be eligible to receive.” The statute puts the ceiling at a maximum of 4 percent. That is a real reduction on a real bill and it has nothing to do with appealing — it is for paying early.

What to do in the next hour

  1. Find the sentence on page two with ON OR BEFORE in it. Write that date somewhere you will see it.
  2. Compare Column 2 to Column 3. If the gap is what upset you, your fight is at the hearing in column seven, and it is not a petition.
  3. Look at market value on page two. Ask one question honestly: would it have sold for that on 1 January? If no, you have a petition, and the clock started when the notice was mailed.
  4. Check that every exemption you are entitled to is listed. A missing homestead is the fastest win there is at a VAB hearing, and it is also the easiest thing to fix by phone before you ever file.
